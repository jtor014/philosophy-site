In our last episode, we stood in the mud with Aristotle and watched him
invent the scientific method. We watched him look at the world around
him---not as a shadow of some perfect reality in the sky, but as
something real. Something to be studied.

We talked about **Teleology**---the idea that everything in nature has a
purpose. The acorn has a purpose: to become the oak. The stone has a
purpose: to fall toward the center of the earth.

But today, we have to apply that rigorous, scientific method to the most
complex, messy, and confusing organism in the entire catalog.

**Us.**

Today we ask the question: If the eye has a function---to see\... and if
a knife has a function---to cut\... then what is the function of a Human
Being?

We are leaving the realm of Physics, and we are entering the realm of
Ethics. But don\'t be fooled---Aristotle doesn\'t change his personality
just because the topic changed. He doesn\'t suddenly become a
sentimental poet.

To understand his answer, we're going to fast forward. We're going to
leave the young, vigorous scientist in the lagoon, and visit a dying man
in a dimly lit room in 322 BC. We're going to look at his Last Will and
Testament to discover that for Aristotle, the \'Good Life\' isn\'t a
theory you memorize. It's a practice.

So, put down the microscope, and pick up the mirror.

What does it mean for a human being to flourish?\"

I want you to place yourself in a dimly lit room on the island of
Euboea. The year is 322 BC. The air is thick with the salt of the
Aegean, but inside, the atmosphere is heavy with something else:
finality.

Lying on a bed is a man of sixty-two. He is not the rugged, barefoot
street-brawler that Socrates was. He is a man of fashion---or at least
he was. He wears rings on his fingers---his hair is cut short---he is
surrounded by fine linens. This is Aristotle, the man who tutored
Alexander the Great, the man who built the library that categorized the
world.

But he is not in Athens. He is in exile. Just like his predecessor
Socrates, the mood of the city turned against him. But unlike Socrates,
Aristotle didn\'t stay to drink the hemlock. He famously said he would
not allow the Athenians to \"sin twice against philosophy\". He chose
survival. He chose life.

And now, at the very end of that life, he is dictating a document.

Now, you might be thinking that the \"Master of Those Who Know,\" the
inventor of logic, the man who systematized biology, physics, and
metaphysics, would leave behind a final intellectual manifesto. You
might expect a last, cold syllogism to solve the riddle of the universe.

But he doesn't. He dictates a will.

And this document, preserved for us by the biographer Diogenes Laertius,
is shocking. Not because of its grandeur, but because of its tenderness.
It dismantles the caricature of the cold, distant philosopher.

Listen to what he says. He doesn\'t talk about the Unmoved Mover or the
logic of categories. He talks about the people he loves. He issues
instructions for the care of Herpyllis, his concubine. Why? He dictates
the reason quite simply: \"Because she has been good to me\".

Think about that phrase. The man who defined the laws of the universe
pauses to record a simple human kindness. He ensures she is taken care
of, that she has a home, that she is secure.

He then turns his attention to his slaves. In a world where slaves were
considered \"living tools\", Aristotle orders that his slaves be freed.
He doesn\'t treat them as property to be liquidated, but as humans to be
liberated.

And finally, he makes a request that bridges the gap between the living
and the dead. He asks that the bones of his first wife, Pythias, be
disinterred and buried alongside him, just as she had requested. Even in
death, he seeks the company of his \"other self\".

Why are we starting here? Why start a philosophy episode about Ethics
and Politics with a deathbed legal document?

Because this is the philosophy.

If Plato was the philosopher of the \"City of Words\"---that perfect,
abstract utopia in the sky---Aristotle is the philosopher of the \"City
of Deeds\". His ethics are not about escaping our humanity---they are
about inhabiting it fully.

Well, that\'s sentimental, but what does it have to do with moral
philosophy?

It has everything to do with it. Aristotle's entire ethical project is
driven by a single, biological question: What does it mean for this
specific animal---the human animal---to flourish?

When we look at this will, we see the answer in action. We see a man who
valued friendship, who valued family, who valued the material security
of those he loved. We see a man who understood that \"Happiness\" isn\'t
a feeling you have inside your head---it is a web of relationships and
actions you build in the world.

Aristotle understood that you cannot be happy if you are starving. He
understood you cannot be happy if you are alone. He understood that the
\"Good Life\" requires luck, leisure, and love.

So, as we stand over the dying philosopher in Chalcis, we are witnessing
the final argument of his life. He is showing us that wisdom is not
about knowing the definition of \"Good.\" It is about being good to
Herpyllis because she was good to you.

Today, we are going to dive into the manual he wrote for this life. We
are going to open the Nicomachean Ethics and the Politics. We are going
to look at the tools he gave us to navigate the messy, violent,
beautiful reality of being a human being.

But to do that, we have to clear up a linguistic confusion. We have to
talk about a word that we have been mistranslating for two thousand
years.

The word is \"Happiness.\"

If you open a standard translation of Aristotle's Nicomachean
Ethics---the book named after his son, Nicomachus, who likely edited his
father's lecture notes---you will read that the ultimate goal of human
life is \"Happiness\".

And immediately, you are likely to misunderstand him.

When we say \"happiness\" in the twenty-first century, we are talking
about a mood. We are talking about a psychological state. It is the
feeling you get when you eat a warm cookie, or when your team wins the
Super Bowl, or when you fall in love. It is a spike in dopamine. It is
subjective. If you feel happy, you are happy. No one can tell you
otherwise.

But Aristotle is not talking about warm cookies.

The Greek word he uses is Eudaimonia. And if we break it down
philologically, it doesn\'t mean \"smiling.\" Eu means \"good\" or
\"well.\" Daimon refers to a spirit or a minor deity. Literally, it
means having a \"good spirit\" watching over you, or being in a state of
\"good spiritedness.\"

But a better translation---one that captures the sweat and grit of
Aristotle's worldview---is Flourishing. Or even better: Success.

Think of it this way. Imagine you are a biologist looking at a
houseplant in the corner of your room.

If you want to know if that plant is \"happy\" in the Aristotelian
sense, you don\'t ask it how it feels. You don\'t look for a smile. You
look at its leaves. Are they green and reaching toward the light? Are
the roots deep? is it fulfilling its potential as a plant? If it is
withering, brown, and dry, it is not flourishing, regardless of whether
it \"thinks\" it's doing great.

For Aristotle, a human life is exactly the same. Eudaimonia is not a
mood ring---it is a career assessment. It is an objective judgment about
the quality of your entire life.

Now, you might be thinking, \"That sounds incredibly harsh. Who are you
to tell me I\'m not happy if I feel happy? If I want to sit on the
couch, eat Cheetos, and play video games for forty years, and I enjoy
every minute of it, who is Aristotle to say I'm not living the Good
Life?\"

Aristotle would look at you, perhaps adjust his fine linen robe, and
say: \"You are confusing the life of a human with the life of a grazing
cow\".

He actually runs through a list of candidates for the \"Good Life\" and
dismisses them one by one, using a logical process of elimination:

It cannot be Pleasure, because that is fit for cattle, not humans.

It cannot be Wealth, because money is only useful for buying other
things. It is a means, not an end.

It cannot even be Honor (political fame), which was the obsession of the
Greeks. Why? Because honor depends on the people giving it, not the
person receiving it. If your happiness depends on the applause of the
crowd, you are a slave to their opinion.

No, Eudaimonia must be something self-sufficient. It must be an
activity, not a passive state. You cannot be \"flourishing\" while you
are asleep. You have to be doing something.

But there is a darker implication to this definition. If happiness is an
objective assessment of a whole life, then you cannot actually know if
you are \"happy\" until the very end.

Aristotle brings up a famous story involving Solon, the wise Athenian
lawgiver. Solon famously warned the wealthy King Croesus: \"Call no man
happy until he is dead\".

At first glance, this sounds absurd. It sounds like something a
depressed teenager would write in a journal. But look at it through the
lens of Eudaimonia.

If happiness is \"Success\"---if it is a story with a beginning, middle,
and end---then a tragic ending can retroactively ruin the story.

Aristotle points to Priam, the King of Troy. For seventy years, Priam
lived the perfect life. He was rich, powerful, respected, and had many
children. He was flourishing. But in his final years, he watched his
city burn, his sons die, and his wife dragged away into slavery before
he was butchered on his own altar.

We cannot look at Priam's life and say, \"Well, he was happy for the
first seventy years, so it balances out.\" No. The tragedy of the ending
shatters the Eudaimonia of the whole.

This is terrifying. It means your happiness is not entirely under your
control. It is fragile. It requires luck. It requires external goods.
Aristotle is the realist who admits that you cannot be happy if you are
being tortured on a rack, no matter how virtuous you are. The \"City of
Words\" might be immune to fire, but the \"City of Deeds\" burns.

So, if Eudaimonia is this high bar---this total, flourishing success of
a human life---how do we achieve it? If we aren\'t just supposed to
chase pleasure or money, what is the actual target?

To answer that, Aristotle stops acting like a philosopher and starts
acting like a biologist. He stops looking at the clouds and starts
dissecting the organism. He asks a simple, cutting question:

What is a human being for?

We have established that the goal of human life is
Eudaimonia---Flourishing. We know it isn\'t just a mood---it's a career
success. It is a life that works.

But this immediately creates a massive practical problem.

If you want to build a \"good\" table, you have a blueprint. If you want
to bake a \"good\" cake, you have a recipe. But if you want to build a
\"good life,\" where are the instructions? There is no IKEA manual for
being human.

Or is there?

Aristotle believes there is. But to find it, he stops acting like a
philosopher gazing at the heavens, and starts acting like a biologist
dissecting a specimen. He believes that if you want to know what the
\"Good\" is for any organism, you have to ask one specific question:

What is it for?

This brings us to the bedrock of his ethical system: The Function
Argument.

The Greek word here is Ergon. It translates to \"Function,\" \"Task,\"
or \"Work.\"

Aristotle looks around the world and sees that everything in nature has
an Ergon. The function of a knife is to cut. The function of an eye is
to see. The function of an acorn is to become an oak tree.

Now, how do we judge if these things are \"Good\"?

Simple. A \"Good Knife\" is one that cuts well. A \"Bad Knife\" is one
that is dull and crushes the tomato instead of slicing it. A \"Good
Eye\" sees 20/20. A \"Bad Eye\" is blind. The \"Goodness\" isn\'t some
magical aura added on top---the Goodness is the successful performance
of the Function.

So, Aristotle turns his gaze to us. He asks a question that is both
simple and devastating:

\"Has the carpenter and the shoemaker a function, but Man has none? Is
he born without a work?\"

It seems absurd to think that your thumb has a function (to grasp), and
your eyelid has a function (to protect the eye), but You---the whole
organism---are just a random accident with no job description.

Aristotle refuses to accept that. \"Nature does nothing in vain.\"

So, if we have a function, what is it?

To find it, Aristotle uses a process of elimination. He peels back the
layers of biological life to find the one thing that is unique to
humans.

First, is it Living? Well, plants live. They take in nutrients, they
grow, they reproduce. If your \"Function\" is just to eat, sleep, and
get bigger, then you are aspiring to be a successful fern. Aristotle
dismisses this. That is the life of a vegetable, not a human.

Second, is it Feeling? Well, cows and horses feel pain and pleasure.
They have eyes and ears. They chase after food and run from wolves. If
your \"Function\" is just to chase pleasure and avoid pain, you are
aspiring to be a successful cow. Aristotle dismisses this too. That is
the life of a beast.

So, what is left?

There is one capacity that separates the human from the fern and the
cow. Reason. (Logos).

Humans are the only animals that can think abstractly, use language, and
deliberate about the future. We are the \"Rational Animal.\" Therefore,
Aristotle concludes, the specific Ergon of a human being is \"an
activity of the soul in accordance with Reason.\"

But wait. It's not enough just to have reason. You have to use it well.

This brings us to one of the most important words in the entire Greek
vocabulary: Arete.

We usually translate Arete as Virtue. But \"Virtue\" is a terrible
translation. It sounds Victorian. It sounds like a schoolmarm scolding
you for showing your ankles. It implies sexual purity or just \"being
nice.\"

Arete is much muscular. It means Excellence. It means Functional
Goodness.

A knife that cuts dangerously sharp has Arete. A racehorse that runs
explosively fast has Arete. A soldier who kills efficiently has Arete.

So, when Aristotle talks about a \"Virtuous Man,\" he isn\'t talking
about a man who is merely \"sinless\" or \"pure.\" He is talking about a
High-Performance Human. He is talking about someone who is operating at
the peak of their potential.

He uses the metaphor of the Flute Player.

If you hand a flute to a random person, they might blow into it and make
a noise. They are \"using\" the flute. But that is not the goal. The
goal is to play the flute well. The goal is to make music.

For Aristotle, your life is the flute. Your Reason is the breath. And
the \"Good Life\" is the music that comes out when you play it with
Arete.

Now, you might be thinking: \"This sounds incredibly elitist. Are you
saying that only \'smart\' people can be good? If my function is Reason,
does that mean a PhD in Physics is \'better\' than a kind-hearted
janitor?\"

This is a crucial objection. And Aristotle's answer is surprising.

He distinguishes between two types of Reason: Theoretical Reason and
Practical Reason.

Theoretical Reason is the PhD stuff. Understanding the stars, math, and
logic.

Practical Reason is the ability to guide your actions and emotions
correctly.

The \"Good Life\" isn\'t just about thinking deep thoughts. It is about
obeying Reason in your actions. It means getting angry, but only when it
is reasonable to be angry. It means eating, but only the amount that
reason dictates is healthy.

The janitor who manages his life with discipline, raises his children
well, and treats his neighbors with justice is fulfilling his human
function better than the brilliant physicist who is an alcoholic and
cheats on his taxes. The janitor is playing the human flute with
excellence---the physicist is just making noise.

But there is one final, daunting condition to this argument. Aristotle
adds that this activity must happen \"in a complete life.\"

\"For one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day.\"

You cannot call yourself a \"Good Human\" because you did one nice thing
on a Tuesday. Just as you wouldn\'t call a guy a \"Great Football
Player\" because he caught one pass in high school, you cannot judge a
human life by a single moment.

Arete is not an act---it is a habit. It is a consistent, grinding, daily
performance of excellence that lasts from the moment you gain reason
until the moment you die.

This is terrifying. It means the \"Good Life\" is a marathon that you
never stop running. It puts a tremendous weight on consistency.

So, if the goal is to perform this function---to live according to
reason with excellence---how do we actually do it? If we are the Flute
Player, how do we learn the notes? If we are the Archer, where is the
target?

To answer that, Aristotle hands us his most famous tool. He hands us a
way to navigate the chaos of the world without losing our way. He hands
us The Mean.

So, we have established that the \"function\" of a human is to use
Reason well. But that is still abstract. If I am standing in a
boardroom, or arguing with my spouse, or deciding whether to buy a new
car, what does \"using reason well\" actually look like?

Aristotle hands us a navigational tool. It is perhaps the most famous
concept in all of ethics: The Golden Mean (Mesotes).

He argues that moral goodness is almost always a target located between
two extremes. On one side, you have Excess (Too Much). On the other
side, you have Deficiency (Too Little). Virtue is the bullseye in the
middle.

Imagine an Archer standing before a target. There are a million ways to
miss. You can shoot too high, too low, too far left, or into the dirt.
But there is only one way to hit the center.

Aristotle says: \"Men are good in but one way, but bad in many.\"

This creates a terrifying realization: It is much easier to be bad than
to be good. Disorder is the default setting of the universe---Order
requires precision.

Let's apply this \"Concept, then Concrete\" sandwich to the real
emotions we deal with every day.

First, look at Fear and Confidence. If you have too little fear (or too
much confidence), you are Rash. You are the guy who drives 120mph
without a seatbelt. You aren\'t brave---you\'re an idiot. If you have
too much fear, you are a Coward. You are paralyzed by risk. The Mean is
the right amount of fear, for the right reason, at the right time. That
is Courage.

Second, look at Pleasure and Pain. If you indulge every appetite, you
are Self-Indulgent (Intemperate). You are a slave to your belly. If you
reject all pleasure and live like a monk who hates joy, you are
Insensible. You are numb. The Mean is enjoying pleasure but not being
ruled by it. That is Temperance.

Third, look at Social Interaction. The person who jokes about
everything, even at a funeral, is a Buffoon. The person who cannot take
a joke and is sour to everyone is Boorish. The Mean is the person who is
funny but tactful. That is Wittiness.

Now, you might be thinking, \"Okay, so I just calculate the average? If
goodness is math, do I just act lukewarm about everything?\"

No. Aristotle is very careful here. He says the Mean is \"relative to
us.\"

He uses the example of Milo the Wrestler (the Arnold Schwarzenegger of
ancient Greece). If a trainer is deciding how much food Milo should eat,
\"Too Much\" might be 20 pounds of meat, and \"Too Little\" might be 2
pounds. The \"Mean\" for Milo is 10 pounds.

But if you or I eat 10 pounds of meat, we die of gluttony.

The \"right amount\" depends entirely on who you are and what the
situation demands. If a terrorist enters the room, the \"mean\" of Anger
is high---you should be enraged. If a waiter forgets your straw, the
\"mean\" of Anger is zero. Morality isn\'t a rigid rulebook---it is a
calibration.

But Aristotle is a realist. He knows we aren\'t born in the middle. We
are all born with natural tilts. Some of us are naturally
anxious---others are naturally hot-headed.

So, how do we fix ourselves? He offers a psychological hack known as The
Bent Stick Method.

Imagine a piece of wood that is warped and curved hard to the left. If
you want to straighten it, you don\'t just bend it to the middle---it
will snap back. You have to bend it all the way to the extreme right.
You have to overcorrect.

If you know you are a Coward, you shouldn\'t aim for \"Caution.\" You
should aim for \"Rashness.\" You force yourself to do things that feel
terrifyingly risky. If you know you are a Glutton, you shouldn\'t aim
for \"Dieting.\" You should aim for \"Fasting.\"

You have to drag yourself to the opposite extreme, and eventually, your
nature will settle back into the middle.

This brings us to the engine room of the Aristotelian system. If there
is no rulebook---if the \"Mean\" changes based on the person and the
situation---how on earth do we know where the target is?

We need a specific kind of intelligence. Aristotle calls it Phronesis.
We translate this as Practical Wisdom.

Aristotle distinguishes this from Sophia (Theoretical Wisdom). Sophia is
knowing that E=mc² or that the angles of a triangle add up to 180
degrees. It is universal and unchanging. Phronesis is knowing that \"My
boss is in a bad mood, so I shouldn\'t ask for a raise today.\" It is
particular and changing.

We all know people who have high Sophia but low Phronesis. The brilliant
professor who can explain quantum mechanics but can\'t figure out how to
talk to his own children. The genius coder who accidentally insults
everyone in the meeting.

For Aristotle, you cannot be a good person without Phronesis. You need
the street smarts to read the room. You need the \"Executive\" part of
the soul that looks at the messy, chaotic data of life and says, \"Here.
The target is here.\"

And the only way to get Phronesis? Experience.

You can\'t learn it in a classroom. You have to fail. You have to shoot
the arrow, miss, adjust, and shoot again. You have to build the Habit
(Hexis).

\"We become builders by building and we become harpists by playing the
harp. Similarly, then, we become just by doing just acts.\"

So, if we practice this for a lifetime---if we bend the stick, aim for
the mean, and develop our practical wisdom---what is the result? What
does the \"Perfected Human\" actually look like?

He looks like a giant.

We have talked about the Archer. We have talked about the Habit. We have
talked about the messy business of practical wisdom. But now, we have to
look at the result. If you get all of this right---if you hit the
bullseye of Courage, Temperance, and Justice over and over again for a
lifetime---what do you become?

Aristotle doesn\'t just say \"you become a good guy.\" He paints a
portrait of a specific character type. He introduces us to the
Megalopsychos.

In Greek, Mega means \"Great\" and Psyche means \"Soul.\" This is the
Great-Souled Man.

And I need to warn you: when you meet him, you probably aren\'t going to
like him.

If you were raised in the West anytime in the last two thousand years,
your moral compass has likely been calibrated by Christianity. You value
humility. You value the idea of the \"Servant Leader.\" You think the
person at the top should act like they are at the bottom.

Aristotle would find that ridiculous.

For Aristotle, Humility is a vice. He calls it \"Small-Souledness\"
(Mikropsychia). It is the error of not knowing your own worth. If you
are excellent, and you act like a nobody, you aren\'t being \"nice.\"
You are being ignorant. You are failing to recognize reality.

The Great-Souled Man is the \"Crown of the Virtues.\" He is the man who
possesses all the other virtues we've discussed---courage, honesty,
magnificence---and because of that, he deems himself worthy of great
things. And here is the crucial part: He is right.

Aristotle gives us a description of this man that is so specific it
feels like he is describing a real person---maybe himself, or maybe an
idealized version of a Spartan king.

He tells us the Great-Souled Man moves slowly. Why? Because there is
nothing in the world important enough to make him rush. He has a deep,
resonant voice. He speaks clearly and deliberately because he isn\'t
anxious about whether you agree with him. He is totally self-contained.

He does not ask for favors. To ask for help is to admit a deficiency.
But he loves bestowing favors. If you do something nice for him, he will
immediately do something even nicer for you in return.

Now, you might be thinking, \"This sounds petty. Is he just trying to
one-up me?\"

No. He is doing it to restore the balance of superiority. He refuses to
be in your debt. He wants to be the source of good, not the recipient of
it.

This is the \"Alien Aristocrat\" moment. We are staring directly into
the face of a pagan value system. This is a morality for the strong, for
the victors, for the men who built the Parthenon. It is an ethic of
Pride.

But before we dismiss him as a narcissist, we have to grapple with the
kernel of truth Aristotle is protecting here. He is asking: Is false
modesty really a virtue?

If you are the best surgeon in the world, and you say, \"Oh, I\'m okay,
I guess,\" you are lying. And for Aristotle, truth is more important
than politeness. The Great-Souled Man is obsessed with the Truth. He is
open in his hatreds and open in his loves. He refuses to gossip, he
refuses to complain, and he refuses to flatter. He walks through the
world with a kind of terrifying honesty, because he doesn\'t need
anyone\'s approval.

He is like a god walking among mortals.

But this creates a massive problem. If the Great-Souled Man is so
self-sufficient, if he is so complete, does he need anyone else? If he
is a god, surely he is happy alone on his mountaintop?

Aristotle stops, looks at the logic, and says: No.

In one of the most tender twists in the Ethics, Aristotle argues that
even the Superman needs a friend.

\"No one would choose to live without friends, even if he had all the
other goods.\"

Think about that. You could have the wealth of Croesus, the power of
Alexander, and the virtue of Socrates. But if you are alone, the \"Good
Life\" is mathematically impossible.

This brings us to Philia.

We translate this as \"Friendship,\" but the Greek concept is wider. It
includes the bond between business partners, the bond between fellow
soldiers, and the bond between husband and wife.

Aristotle, ever the biologist, dissects Friendship into three distinct
species. He puts them under the microscope to see what holds them
together.

First, there is the Friendship of Utility. This is the friendship of the
marketplace. I am friends with you because you sell me grain---you are
friends with me because I pay you silver. The Glue is Usefulness. The
Flaw is that it is fragile. As soon as the deal is done, or as soon as I
find a cheaper grain merchant, the friendship dissolves. It is purely
transactional. Aristotle notes this is common among the old, who are
practical.

Second, there is the Friendship of Pleasure. This is the friendship of
the young. This is your drinking buddy. This is the person you play
rugby with, or the person you gossip with. You love them because they
are fun. The Glue is Pleasure. The Flaw is that our tastes change. The
jokes that were funny at twenty are annoying at forty. When the fun
stops, the friendship stops.

Now, pause. You might be looking at your own social circle and thinking,
\"That's about 95% of the people I know.\"

And Aristotle would say, \"Yes. That is the human condition.\"

But there is a third kind. The rare kind. The Friendship of Virtue.

This happens between two people who are good in themselves. In this
relationship, I don\'t love you for what you give me. I don\'t love you
for how you make me feel. I love you for who you are.

I look at your character---your Arete---and I recognize it as beautiful.
And you look at mine and see the same.

Aristotle calls this friend \"Another Self\" (Allos Autos).

This is a profound psychological insight. When you have a true friend,
their happiness becomes your happiness. You aren\'t competing. You
aren\'t trading. You are expanding your own soul to include them.

But here is the catch: Bad people cannot have this kind of friendship.

Why? Because bad people are inconsistent. They don\'t even like
themselves---they are full of regret and conflict. If you don\'t like
yourself, how can you be a \"second self\" to someone else?

Only the virtuous can be true friends. And this friendship is permanent,
because character is permanent.

But it is also the hardest work. Aristotle drily notes that you cannot
really know someone until you have \"eaten a peck of salt together.\" It
takes time. It takes shared suffering. It takes the long, boring grind
of living side-by-side.

This is why you cannot have 100 \"best friends.\" You physically do not
have the time \"to be good\" to that many people. True friendship is an
exclusive club.

Now, before we leave the realm of relationships, we have to mention a
ghost.

There is a massive piece of Aristotle's work that we are skipping over
today, but I have to flag it because it connects to this idea of
emotional health.

Aristotle wrote a book called the Poetics. It is the manual for how to
tell a story. And in it, he argues that we need Art---specifically
Tragedy---to cleanse our emotions. He calls this Catharsis.

Just as the body builds up toxins that need to be purged, the soul
builds up pity and fear. We need to go to the theater, watch a tragedy
(like Oedipus Rex), and cry our eyes out. We need to feel that release
so we can go back to being rational citizens.

For Aristotle, the \"Good Life\" isn\'t just about logic. It requires
the deep emotional bonds of Friendship and the deep emotional release of
Art. He is building a complete ecosystem for the human animal.

But you and your friend don\'t live in a vacuum. You don\'t live in a
theater. You live in a City.

And that brings us to the final, and most dangerous, layer of the onion.
We have to talk about Politics.

We've established that you can't practice virtue alone. We've
established that you need friends---\"Another Self\"---to practice the
habits of the Good Life.

But you and your friends don't live in a vacuum. You don't live on a
cloud. You live here, in the mud and the noise of the City.

And this brings us to the opening of Aristotle's Politics, and perhaps
the most famous definition in the history of western thought.

\"Man is by nature a political animal.\"

Now, hold on. When we hear \"Political Animal\" in the 21st century, we
think of a guy in a suit kissing babies and lying about taxes. We think
of House of Cards.

That is not what Aristotle means. The Greek phrase is Zoon Politikon.
Zoon means \"Animal\" or \"Living Thing.\" Politikon means \"of the
Polis\"---of the City-State.

He is saying that human beings are Habitat-Specific Animals. Just as a
fish is designed to swim in water, and a bird is designed to fly in air,
a human is designed to live in a Polis.

Why?

Aristotle looks at biology again. He notes that nature gives other
animals a Voice (Phone) to express pain and pleasure. A dog can yelp if
you step on its tail. It can communicate \"Ouch\" or \"Yum.\"

But nature gave humans something else. Nature gave us Speech (Logos).

Speech isn't just for making noise. Speech is for declaring what is
advantageous and what is harmful; what is just and what is unjust.

And you cannot practice Justice alone. You can't be \"fair\" to a rock.
You can't be \"generous\" to a tree. You need a community.

Therefore, Aristotle concludes, the City is not just a convenient way to
organize trash collection and defense. The City is the soul writ large.
It is the only place where we can fully become human.

And then he drops the hammer, a line that should make every \"rugged
individualist\" uncomfortable:

\"He who is unable to live in society, or who has no need because he is
sufficient for himself, must be either a beast or a god.\"

If you live outside the City, you are either sub-human (a wild animal
who cannot speak) or super-human (a god who needs nothing). But you are
not a man.

So, if the City is our natural habitat, how do we organize it?

Plato, Aristotle\'s teacher, tried to solve this by imagining a perfect
Utopia in his head---the Republic.

Aristotle, ever the biologist, takes a different approach. He doesn\'t
dream; he collects data. He and his students collected 158 actual
constitutions from around the Mediterranean. He looked at how power
actually works, not how it should work.

And what he found was a pattern. A cycle of decay.

He classifies governments based on two simple variables: Who rules?
(One, Few, or Many). And who do they rule for? (The Common Good or Their
Own Interest).

This gives us six types of government. Three are healthy, and three are
diseased.

First, the Rule of One. The Healthy version is Monarchy. A wise King
rules like a father, caring for his children. The Diseased version is
Tyranny. The King stops caring about the people and treats the City as
his personal bank account. This is the worst regime, because the
corruption is concentrated in a single, powerful hand.

Second, the Rule of the Few. The Healthy version is Aristocracy.
Literally, the \"Rule of the Best\" (Aristos). These are the virtuous,
the experts, the people with Phronesis. The Diseased version is
Oligarchy. The \"Rule of the Rich.\" They stop ruling for the city and
start ruling for their wallets, using the law to grind down the poor.

Third, the Rule of the Many. The Healthy version is Polity (Politeia).
This is a constitutional government where the citizens rule and are
ruled in turn, respecting the law. The Diseased version is Democracy.

Wait. Did I just say Democracy is the diseased version?

Yes.

For Aristotle, \"Democracy\" didn\'t mean \"Liberty and Justice for
All.\" It meant Mob Rule.

Remember, Aristotle was not a fan of the \"wisdom of the crowd.\" He had
seen the crowd in action. He knew that a jury of 501 Athenians had voted
to kill Socrates. He knew that the poor, if given absolute power, would
not be \"just.\" They would simply use their numbers to loot the rich. A
pure Democracy is a regime of passion, not reason. It is a beast with a
thousand heads.

So, how do we stop the slide into chaos? How do we prevent the Polity
from becoming a Mob, or the Aristocracy from becoming an Oligarchy?

Aristotle places his bet on the Middle Class.

He argues that the very rich are too arrogant to obey the law, and the
very poor are too desperate to rule well. But the Middle Class---those
who possess a \"moderate and sufficient property\"---are the ballast of
the ship. They are the \"Golden Mean\" of the political world. They
don\'t plot against the rich (because they have some property
themselves), and they don\'t exploit the poor (because they aren\'t
powerful enough to get away with it).

A stable city is a city with a massive, boring, law-abiding middle.

But even this solution has a dark side. Aristotle's vision of the \"Good
City\" relies on a specific economic engine. It relies on the idea that
some people have the leisure to be virtuous because others are doing the
dirty work.

And this brings us to the Shadow. We cannot leave Aristotle without
confronting the monster in the basement of the Lyceum.

We cannot sanitize Aristotle. If we want the man, we have to take the
monster in the basement, too.

In Book I of the Politics, Aristotle makes an argument that chills the
modern blood. He argues for the necessity of \"Natural Slavery\".

He looks at the economy of the ancient world---which ran entirely on
forced labor---and he doesn\'t see a crime. He sees biology.

He defines a slave in a way that is terrified in its cold precision: \"A
slave is a living tool\" (Empsychon Organon). Just as a hammer is an
inanimate tool for driving nails, a slave is an animate tool for
managing the household.

But how does he justify this? He doesn\'t say \"might makes right.\" He
uses his favorite weapon: Teleology.

He argues that some human beings are \"marked out for subjection\" from
the moment of birth. He claims that while these people have strong
bodies fit for labor, their souls lack the rational part that creates
virtue. They can perceive reason (enough to follow orders), but they
cannot generate it.

Therefore, Aristotle concludes with a horrifying logical calmness, it is
actually \"better\" for them to be ruled by a master, just as it is
better for the body to be ruled by the soul, or the sheep to be ruled by
the shepherd.

Now, you might be screaming at your speakers. You might be thinking,
\"How? How could the man who invented Logic, the man who wrote the
Ethics, be this blind?\"

This is the Naturalistic Fallacy. Aristotle looked at the world as it
was---a world where Greeks dominated \"barbarians\" in war---and he
assumed this social reality was a biological necessity. He mistook
History (who won the war) for Nature (who has the better soul).

This is a stark warning for us. Aristotle was a genius. He was smarter
than you, and he was smarter than me. And yet, he could not see past the
prejudices of his own zip code. It forces us to ask: What are the
\"Natural Slaves\" of our time? What are the evils we accept today as
\"just the way the world works,\" which our grandchildren will look back
on with horror?

But Aristotle is not done with the household (Oikos). While he failed on
slavery, he scored a massive victory on the subject of the Family.

Remember Plato? In the Republic, Plato wanted to abolish the traditional
family for his Guardian class. He wanted children to be raised
communally, so that no father would know his own son, and every citizen
would love the City equally.

Aristotle looks at this \"Utopia\" and shakes his head. He argues that
Plato doesn\'t understand human nature.

He says: \"What is common to the greatest number has the least care
bestowed upon it\".

This is the first articulation of what we now call the \"Tragedy of the
Commons.\"

Aristotle argues that we love things because they are our own. If
everyone is my son, then no one is my son. If the whole city is my
family, then the love becomes watered down, like a drop of sweet wine in
a gallon of water.

He defends private property and the private family not because he is a
capitalist, but because he believes that responsibility requires
ownership. You water your own garden; you don\'t water the public park.

We have walked through the Ethics and the Politics. We have dissected
the Archer, the Friend, and the Citizen.

But you might be sitting there, perhaps driving in your car or washing
dishes, thinking: \"This is all very interesting history, but does it
actually work? We live in a world of quantum mechanics, liberal
democracy, and Tinder. What can a guy who thought the brain was a
radiator possibly teach us about the Good Life?\"

The answer is: He can teach us why we are so miserable.

In 1981, the philosopher Alasdair MacIntyre wrote a book called After
Virtue. It begins with a science fiction scenario. Imagine a series of
environmental disasters are blamed on scientists. Mobs burn down
laboratories, lynch physicists, and destroy textbooks. Science is wiped
out.

Generations later, people try to rebuild. They find fragments of the old
books. They find words like \"Neutron\" and \"Mass\" and \"Gravity.\"
They use these words in arguments, but they have completely lost the
theoretical context that gave them meaning. They are using the
vocabulary of science, but they aren\'t doing science. They are
performing a ritual.

MacIntyre says: This is us.

But it isn\'t science we lost. It is Morality.

We use words like \"Rights,\" \"Justice,\" \"Fairness,\" and \"Duty.\"
We shout them at each other on cable news and Twitter. But we never
agree. The arguments never end. Why?

Because we have lost the Telos.

Remember the Bridge.

Aristotle's system was a bridge between Man-as-he-is (a messy, emotional
animal) and Man-as-he-could-be (if he realized his essential nature).
The bridge consisted of the Virtues. You practice the virtues to cross
the bridge.

But during the Enlightenment, we blew up the destination. We decided
that nature doesn\'t have a purpose. There is no \"Function of Man.\"
There is just biology and atoms.

So now, we are standing on the bridge, holding a map of rules, but the
other side of the canyon is gone. We are following the
instructions---\"Don\'t lie,\" \"Be fair\"---but we have no idea why. We
are like a football team running plays on a field with no end zones. We
are playing the game, but we have lost the victory.

Aristotle invites us to stop running. He invites us to rebuild the
destination. He asks us to stop asking \"What are the rules?\" and start
asking \"What kind of person do I want to become?\"

And this brings us, finally, back to where we started.

I want you to return to that dimly lit room in Chalcis, 322 BC.

The air is still heavy with the salt of the Aegean. The breathing of the
old man is shallow now. The scroll is finished. The ink is dry.

We started this episode by looking at his will. We were surprised that
the great logician, the man of categories and syllogisms, spent his
final breath ensuring that Herpyllis---his concubine---would be taken
care of. We were touched that he freed his slaves. We were moved by his
request to be buried with the bones of Pythias, his first wife.

But now, after walking through the Ethics, we see this document
differently.

This isn\'t just a list of assets. It is a final proof of concept.

Aristotle is not dying in the \"City of Words.\" He is not dying in a
theoretical Utopia where wives are held in common and philosopher-kings
rule from the clouds.

He is dying in the \"City of Deeds.\"

He provides for Herpyllis not because of a categorical imperative, but
because of Friendship (Philia). He remembers her kindness---\"because
she has been good to me\"---and he reciprocates, restoring the balance,
just as the Great-Souled Man would.

He frees his slaves, perhaps realizing in the twilight of his life that
a \"living tool\" is still a human soul capable of gratitude.

He asks for Pythias because even the most self-sufficient man, even the
god-like philosopher, cannot be happy alone. He needs his \"Other
Self.\"

He spent his life categorizing the universe---from the intestines of a
dogfish to the movements of the stars. But in the end, he didn\'t leave
us a formula. He left us a challenge.

He showed us that the \"Good Life\" is not something you discover; it is
something you practice. It is a habit you build, arrow by arrow, choice
by choice, until the day you die.

The target is there. The bow is in your hands.

Pick it up.
